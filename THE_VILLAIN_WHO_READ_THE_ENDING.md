# The Villain Who Read the Ending

## Chapter 1 — The Man Who Owned Everything

"Tell him he's lucky I'm even acknowledging the request," Jiang Tingyuan said, not looking up from his tablet. "But the answer is no. He can wait in the lobby until his security badge is deactivated."

The boardroom had the smell of expensive roast coffee and the silent, vibrating energy of victory. Around the mahogany table, executives were practically glowing, celebrating the final absorption of a decade-long rival. To them, it was a historic merger; to Jiang, it was simply the logical conclusion of a hostile takeover. He didn't join in the champagne toasts. He didn't feel a surge of pride. He felt the same clinical satisfaction one feels when a complex equation finally balances to zero.

He stood by the floor-to-ceiling windows, watching the city sprawl beneath him like a circuit board. He remembered the grit of the streets from twenty years ago, the specific, oppressive weight of poverty that teaches a man that mercy is a luxury for the defeated. He had traded a traditional life—wives, children, the warmth of a home—for the cold precision of an empire. He didn't regret the trade. Influence was a far more reliable companion than affection, and the silence of his penthouse was a reward, not a void.

In the back of the town car, Jiang leaned his head against the cool leather and scrolled through the final chapters of a cultivation novel. He scoffed, his thumb flicking aggressively through the pages. The protagonist had just ascended through a series of convenient coincidences and sheer luck, defeating a "mastermind" villain who had suddenly become incompetent for the sake of the plot. "Predictable garbage," Jiang muttered. It was an insult to the concept of strategy. If he had been the one navigating that world, he wouldn't have relied on destiny; he would have acquired the assets, leveraged the weaknesses, and owned the heaven itself before the protagonist even woke up.

The thought was interrupted by a scream of rending metal. A freight truck, having lost its brakes, tore through the intersection and slammed into the passenger side of the car with a violence that defied physics. There was no slow-motion realization, no final word of wisdom. Just a sudden, jarring transition from the absolute control of a global empire to the crushing weight of steel and glass. For the first time in his life, Jiang Tingyuan had encountered a variable he couldn't calculate.

* * *

The first thing Jiang noticed was that he was breathing through a nose that didn't smell like burning rubber and ozone. There was no scream of twisting metal, no sudden weight of a collapsing roof—only a profound, oppressive silence and the scent of sandalwood. He didn't panic; he simply opened his eyes and conducted a rapid inventory. He was lying on a canopy bed draped in heavy, gold-embroidered silks. His hands, resting atop a duvet of unnatural softness, were not the hands of a fifty-year-old corporate titan. They were smooth, pale, and lacked the slight tremor of a man who had spent three decades drinking espresso and fighting board wars.

He sat up slowly, his mind clicking through a checklist of facts. He remembered the freight truck. He remembered the sensation of his ribs snapping like dry kindling. Most importantly, he remembered the novel he had been reading in the backseat of the car. As he looked around the room—at the ornate lacquerware, the floating incense burners, and the sheer opulence of the architecture—the pieces of a puzzle he hadn't known he was solving began to lock into place. He wasn't in a hospital, and he certainly wasn't in the afterlife he had vaguely anticipated.

A flood of foreign information surged forward, though it didn't overwrite his own. It was like reading a briefing file on a new acquisition: names, faces, and a complex web of clan politics. He was Gu Tianxing. He was a "Young Master" of the Gu Clan, a title that, in this world, carried as much weight as a nepotism hire in a Fortune 500 company. The realization hit him with a cold, clinical precision: he had been dropped into the world of the cultivation novel, inhabiting the body of a character designed specifically to be a punching bag for the protagonist.

In the text, Gu Tianxing was a footnote of arrogance—a loud-mouthed foil whose only purpose was to provide the hero with a reason to exercise a newfound technique. He was a disposable villain, a stepping stone whose inevitable downfall was written into the very structure of the plot. For a man like Jiang, who had built an empire from nothing by identifying the weakest link in every chain, the irony was palpable. He had been cast in the role of the loser, a man destined to be crushed by a narrative that demanded his failure.

Jiang leaned back against the silk pillows, his gaze drifting to the ornate ceiling. He didn't feel the urge to scream or the impulse to celebrate; instead, he felt the familiar spark of a challenge. He knew the plot, the hidden treasures, and the eventual arrival of the Azure Dragons, but he also knew that the "script" only worked if the characters acted according to their tropes. He was no longer a reader; he was a stakeholder with a vested interest in survival. Before he could rewrite the ending, however, he needed to determine exactly where he was in the timeline—and how many days he had left before the original Gu Tianxing was scheduled to die.

* * *

Jiang swung his legs over the side of the bed and stood. The floor was cool beneath his bare feet. He took in the room once more: the canopy, the embroidered silks, the lacquerware, the sandalwood incense drifting from its floating burner. The place was lavish, but its furnishings told him little about the danger waiting beyond its walls. He checked his hands, flexed his fingers, then rolled his shoulders and tested his balance. The body felt young and uninjured. That was all he could conclude. He had no intention of mistaking a healthy body for a powerful one.

He searched the room without rushing. The desk held no immediately useful record; the shelves and cabinets offered no clear answer to when he had awakened in the original story. He looked for correspondence, a dated document, anything that might anchor his memories to the present. Nothing he found could establish the timeline with confidence. He resisted the temptation to fill the gap with a guess. A wrong assumption made early could be more dangerous than admitting he knew nothing.

The broad strokes of the novel remained in his mind: the original protagonist's rise, Gu Tianxing's brief role as a disposable obstacle, the opportunities Jiang remembered, and the calamity that would eventually come with the Azure Dragons. But he could not place himself precisely within that sequence. The story had given him an ending for the original Gu Tianxing, not a reliable clock counting down to it. Until he knew where he stood, even a seemingly harmless decision might carry consequences he could not foresee.

Jiang crossed to the door and listened. The room beyond remained quiet for a moment, then he heard footsteps stop outside. He opened the door. A household attendant stood in the corridor, waiting nearby, dressed for service rather than cultivation or combat. Jiang did not recognize the person, but that hardly mattered; someone assigned to attend the Gu Clan's young master was a more useful starting point than a guess made in solitude.

He kept his expression composed. There was no reason to reveal that he was a different man, and no need to imitate the original Gu Tianxing's arrogance before he understood the people around him. He studied the attendant briefly, then spoke in an even voice. "Tell me the date. Then tell me what has happened in the Gu Clan recently."

* * *

“The date, Young Master, is the fourteenth day of the current month.”

Jiang waited for more, but the attendant seemed to consider the question answered. He stood outside the doorway with his hands held neatly before him, neither displaying alarm nor making any effort to fill the silence. Jiang studied him for a moment. The man knew who he was supposed to be attending; whether he knew anything useful was another matter.

“What has happened in the Gu Clan recently?” Jiang asked.

“The clan's affairs have proceeded as usual, Young Master. The elders have been occupied with internal matters, and the household has continued its ordinary business.” The attendant paused, then added, “There have been no developments that would require your immediate attention.”

An answer designed to say very little. Jiang could work with that. He had spent years in boardrooms listening to people bury important information beneath layers of professional courtesy. Sometimes the omissions were more revealing than the words themselves. But he had no reason to assume this attendant was hiding anything. Perhaps the man genuinely knew nothing beyond his duties. Perhaps the original Gu Tianxing rarely bothered asking. Either way, pressing him for secrets would produce little value.

“What about my own affairs? Have I been expected anywhere, or is there anything scheduled for me?”

“Nothing that I have been instructed to inform you of, Young Master.”

Jiang gave a small nod. He had learned three things: the date as this world recorded it, the absence of any immediate matter the attendant considered important, and the limits of what this particular source could tell him. None was enough to establish his place in the novel. He could remember its broad progression, the people who would rise, the opportunities that would appear, and the catastrophe that would eventually threaten the entire world. Yet the more he examined those memories, the more he recognized the gap between knowing the story and knowing the present. He had no reliable chronological anchor, and the date alone meant little without a calendar he understood.

“That will be all for now,” Jiang said.

The attendant bowed and withdrew. Jiang closed the door, then stood quietly in the middle of the room. He had wanted a countdown, something precise enough to turn survival into a schedule. Instead, he had confirmed how little he knew about his immediate circumstances. He would not correct that by guessing. First, he needed to understand the body he had inherited and the position Gu Tianxing occupied within the clan. After that, he could compare what he discovered against his memories of the novel. Jiang turned toward the room again, his expression settling into a cold, deliberate calm. He had lost control once because of a variable he could not predict. He would not make the same mistake by treating an unfamiliar world as if it were already understood.

* * *

The room had given Jiang no answers, and the attendant had given him fewer. Standing alone again, he turned his attention to the one thing he had so far treated as an inconvenient fact: the body itself.

He crossed the room at an unhurried pace, testing each movement without making a performance of it. His balance was different. His steps came more easily, and his limbs answered with a lightness he had not felt in decades. There was no stiffness in his knees, no familiar ache in his back, no lingering exhaustion from long hours spent in meetings and cars. That was useful information, but only the beginning. Feeling young was not the same as being strong. A body could be healthy and still be useless in a fight; it could look impressive and conceal weaknesses that only appeared under pressure.

A tall mirror stood among the furnishings. Jiang stopped before it.

The young man reflected there was unmistakably not Jiang Tingyuan. The face was unfamiliar in the way a stranger's face was unfamiliar, yet the knowledge attached to it was not. Gu Tianxing. Young Master of the Gu Clan. A name with status, a body with unknown capabilities, and a future that—in the novel Jiang had finished reading—ended as an early footnote in someone else's rise.

He studied the reflection without flinching. Youth had returned to him, but not the life he had built. The empire, the offices, the networks of obligation and fear—all of it belonged to a world he could no longer reach. He felt no sudden grief for those losses. He had never mistaken ownership for permanence. What irritated him was the transfer of control: he had been forced into a new position without choosing the terms, and the information available to him was nowhere near sufficient to manage it.

Jiang raised one hand, turned it over, and flexed his fingers. He checked his wrists, shoulders, and range of movement, then took a few careful steps and shifted his weight from one foot to the other. Nothing hurt. Nothing in these simple tests revealed an obvious limitation. They also proved almost nothing about cultivation. The world outside this room might measure strength by principles he had never encountered, and a young body could not be assumed to possess any particular talent merely because its owner carried an important title.

That distinction mattered. In business, a title could open doors, but it could not make a failing company profitable. Reputation was an asset only so long as reality supported it. Here, “Young Master” might grant him privileges, enemies, obligations, or all three. He knew the label; he did not yet know its terms.

He looked away from the mirror and surveyed the room once more, this time not as a bewildered man searching for proof that his situation was real, but as an operator inventorying what lay within reach. The furnishings spoke of wealth. The attendant's manner had suggested familiarity with the role he was expected to perform, not necessarily with the man now inhabiting it. The answers he had received established little beyond the fact that the household was continuing without any urgent matter being brought to his attention.

He could demand a parade of explanations. He could pretend to remember every custom and every face. Either course would create risks before he understood what those risks were. A person who knew nothing could ask questions; a person who pretended to know everything would eventually be tested.

Jiang returned to the mirror for one last look. The original Gu Tianxing had been written as an arrogant fool, a convenient obstacle for a protagonist who needed someone to defeat. Jiang had no intention of preserving that role. But he would not escape it merely by deciding he was smarter than the man who had occupied this body before him. He needed evidence: what this body could do, what the clan expected of him, and what people believed they knew about Gu Tianxing.

He had spent his first moments trying to find a date that would tell him how long he had left. That had been the wrong first question. A deadline was useful only after he understood the situation it governed.

Jiang turned from the mirror and settled on a more immediate objective. Before he made any move against the future he remembered, he would establish the facts of his present life—starting with the capabilities of the body he now possessed and the expectations attached to his name. No grand declaration, no reckless display, and no borrowed confidence. First, he would learn the rules of the board. Then he would decide how to break them.

* * *

He did not leave the room to search for answers at random. If the body belonged to a cultivation clan's young master, then the clan's training grounds were the most direct place to begin learning what that title meant in practice.

Jiang opened the door and stepped into the corridor. A servant passing at the far end stopped at once and bowed. Jiang asked where the clan's training grounds were.

The servant hesitated just long enough to be noticed. “The eastern courtyard, Young Master.”

“Take me there.”

“Yes, Young Master.”

The servant led him through a succession of covered walkways and courtyards. Jiang paid attention to the route, the people they passed, and the way each person reacted when they recognized him. Some bowed quickly and looked away. Others watched him for a moment too long before remembering themselves. No one challenged his presence, but neither did the corridor feel like a place where people were at ease around him.

He filed the reactions away. They might mean respect, apprehension, dislike, or some mixture he could not yet distinguish. A single glance was weak evidence. A pattern, repeated under different circumstances, would be worth more.

The eastern courtyard opened beyond a broad stone threshold. Young men and women were training in scattered groups, their movements sharper and more disciplined than anything Jiang could judge from the room he had awakened in. Some practiced with weapons; others stood in pairs, repeating movements while a few older youths watched. The sounds of footwork and striking wood carried across the courtyard.

Jiang stopped at the edge and observed. He had expected to find a place where the body could be tested, but the sight also gave him something else: a standard against which to measure the identity he had inherited. These people were not merely dressed differently from the world he knew. They were learning a craft whose rules he did not understand, inside a hierarchy where his name apparently mattered before he had done anything to earn it.

Their training began to falter as they noticed him. One pair completed its movement late. A young man lowered his practice weapon and glanced at the others, as if checking whether they had seen the same thing. The courtyard did not fall silent, but its rhythm changed. Jiang had seen the same small fracture in a boardroom when an unexpected executive entered a meeting: everyone kept working, and everyone became aware of the audience.

A youth near the closest practice area stepped forward. He looked Jiang over with open surprise, then gave a shallow salute that was technically respectful and little more.

“Young Master. Are you here to train?”

“I'm here to understand what I can do,” Jiang said.

The youth's expression shifted. Surprise gave way to uncertainty, and then to a cautious interest. “Do you want someone to test you?”

There it was: an immediate opportunity to learn something, with a risk attached. Jiang knew nothing about the body's cultivation, the rules of training, or what this young man might consider a reasonable test. Accepting blindly would be foolish. Refusing merely to avoid looking ignorant would be worse. He needed to control the terms without pretending to know more than he did.

“What would the test involve?” Jiang asked.

The youth seemed even more surprised by the question. Around them, a few conversations had stopped altogether.

“A basic exchange,” he said at last. “Nothing serious. I can show you where you stand.”

Jiang studied him, then glanced across the courtyard. The watching faces offered no reliable answer about whether this was a routine courtesy or an unusual spectacle. He would have to judge the situation as it unfolded.

“Then show me,” Jiang said. “And explain what you're doing as we go.”

The youth's grip tightened slightly around his practice weapon. He had expected something else—mockery, perhaps, or an order—but he stepped into the open space and raised the wooden blade into a ready position.

Jiang walked forward. He had come looking for the first measurable fact about his new life. Now he was about to discover whether the body he had inherited was merely young, or whether it could survive the world that had claimed it.

For the first time since awakening, the next step was no longer an idea. It was standing in front of him, holding a weapon.


## Chapter 2 — The First Measure

The youth moved first.

His wooden blade came forward in a clean, uncomplicated line aimed at Jiang's shoulder. It was not fast enough to be frightening, nor slow enough to ignore. Jiang saw the weapon coming and began to consider whether he should step back, turn aside, or raise his arm—three options, none supported by any knowledge of how people fought in this world.

His body had already chosen.

Jiang's left foot shifted diagonally. His shoulders turned a fraction, letting the blade pass close enough for him to feel the air it displaced. His right hand came up, not to seize the weapon, but to guide its path away from him. The movement was small and economical. By the time the youth's strike had passed, Jiang stood just outside its line, balanced and facing his opponent.

For one brief moment, neither of them moved.

Jiang looked at his own hand.

He had not planned that. He had barely understood what the youth was doing before his body had answered. There had been no conscious sequence of instructions, no calculation of angles or timing. He had seen the blade, and something beneath thought had placed him where he needed to be.

The youth snatched the blade back and immediately lowered its tip. His surprise lasted only an instant before it gave way to alarm. He bowed his head, suddenly conscious of how close the weapon had come to the Gu Clan's young master.

“Again,” Jiang said.

The youth looked up sharply. “Young Master?”

“Again.”

“Yes, Young Master.” He reset his stance with visible care, keeping the blade farther away until Jiang gave no sign of objection. “Please watch my shoulders, Young Master, not only the blade. The movement starts before the weapon reaches you.” He demonstrated the shift slowly, then brought the wooden blade across at a controlled pace.

Jiang watched the shoulders. He understood the instruction in principle. In practice, the weapon's path seemed to demand all his attention. He tried to reproduce the shift he had just made, but consciously forcing the movement made him late. The blade tapped his upper arm.

The youth jerked the blade down and took half a step back. “Forgive me, Young Master. I didn't mean to strike you harder than necessary. I only meant that you would have been hit.”

“I understood.” Jiang rolled his shoulder once. The impact was harmless, but the lesson was not. His first response had come naturally; his attempt to repeat it had failed. Whatever had moved him, he could not summon it on command simply by deciding to.

“Why did I avoid the first strike?” he asked.

The youth stared at him, then glanced around the courtyard as if checking whether anyone else had heard the question. “You moved before I finished the attack, Young Master.”

“That describes what happened. It doesn't explain it.”

The youth swallowed. “I don't know how to explain it better, Young Master. Your step was right, and your balance didn't break. Most people who haven't trained would stumble trying to move like that.” His fingers tightened around the hilt. He seemed afraid that any answer might be taken as insolence, but more afraid of giving the Young Master an answer that was plainly wrong.

Jiang absorbed the answer without letting it show on his face. It was an observation, not a verdict. One successful evasion did not make him skilled, and the youth might have been surprised simply because he expected Gu Tianxing to behave differently. Still, the body had produced a result that Jiang's conscious mind could not account for.

“Show me the attack again,” he said. “Slower. Tell me what changes before you move.”

The youth obeyed. He set his feet, shifted his weight, and let Jiang watch each stage before the blade came across. Jiang studied the sequence as he would a machine whose output he could observe but whose internal workings remained hidden. The youth's shoulders turned first; his weight followed; then the weapon moved. There was a pattern. That did not mean Jiang could use it yet.

They tried again.

This time Jiang anticipated the motion and stepped away too early. The youth halted the strike rather than follow through, but the correction left Jiang off balance. He had read the signal and misunderstood the timing. His heel scraped against the stone as he recovered.

“Too soon, Young Master,” the youth said at once. He stopped the blade before it could follow through and lowered it rather than risk another accidental hit. “If you move before I commit, I can change direction.”

“Then the first movement can be a feint.”

“Yes, Young Master.” He bowed his head a fraction. “I should have explained that first. Forgive me.” His eyes flicked toward Jiang's face before he cautiously added, “You understand the principle, then, Young Master?”

“I can reason it out.” Jiang returned to his starting position. “Reasoning and doing are different problems.”

The youth nodded quickly. “Understood, Young Master.” His posture stayed rigid, and he avoided meeting Jiang's eyes for more than a moment. Jiang seemed to have unsettled him simply by asking questions, though whether the youth feared punishment, humiliation, or some other consequence was not yet clear. Jiang noticed, but did not ask. The reactions around the courtyard were becoming harder to dismiss as mere curiosity. Several trainees had stopped their own practice to watch. A few exchanged quiet words, though Jiang could not hear them clearly enough to infer what they meant.

He had wanted a measurement. Instead, the first exchange had given him two contradictory facts: he had moved with an instinctive precision he could not explain, then failed to reproduce it when he tried to understand it. Neither fact canceled the other.

“Once more,” Jiang said. “This time, don't make it easier for me. Just keep it controlled.”

The youth hesitated before speaking, choosing each word with care. “If you permit it, Young Master, I'll aim for your side. Please keep your balance and try to move out of the line. And—please don't grab the blade, Young Master.”

A practical instruction. Jiang appreciated that more than encouragement.

The wooden weapon came toward him again. He watched the shoulders, the shifting weight, the slight turn that preceded the strike. He tried not to think through every joint and muscle. He let the information arrive, held his ground a fraction longer than before, and moved.

Not perfectly. The blade brushed his sleeve and struck his ribs with a dull wooden knock. But his feet stayed beneath him, and he turned with the impact instead of folding around it. He was still in the exchange. He had not won, but he had learned something that the first clean evasion had concealed: the body could help him, yet it would not spare him the work of learning how to use it.

The youth lowered his weapon a little. The surprise on his face had turned into guarded unease. He glanced at the watching trainees, then back at Jiang, and seemed to debate whether to keep silent.

“Forgive me, Young Master. I shouldn't presume to ask, but… do you truly not remember?”

Jiang kept his breathing steady. “Remember what?”

The youth's face tightened. For a moment he looked as though he regretted speaking at all. “Nothing, Young Master. I spoke out of turn. I only thought—given who you are—that you would already know how to move like that. Please forgive my presumption.”

That was more revealing than the strike.

Jiang had come to test his body, but the youth had expected something from Gu Tianxing before they had exchanged more than a few blows. The expectation might have been based on past experience, reputation, or a mistaken assumption. He did not yet know which. He only knew that the body had acted as though it remembered a lesson his mind had never learned—and someone here seemed to think that lesson should already belong to him.

“That will be enough,” Jiang said.

The youth immediately lowered the weapon and stepped back, bowing his head. “Yes, Young Master.”

The youth lowered the wooden blade fully. Around them, the courtyard's ordinary sounds began to return, though not everyone went back to training at once. Jiang flexed the fingers of his right hand, replaying the first movement in his mind. He could describe what had happened. He could not yet make it happen again at will.

That was his first real measurement. Not strength, not rank, and certainly not mastery. A capacity existed here, buried in reflex and unfamiliar habit. Until he could separate what the body knew from what he himself understood, every test would risk confusing luck with ability.

And judging by the youth's unfinished question, Gu Tianxing's body might have a history that its new occupant had not yet learned to read.
