# The Villain Who Read the Ending

## Chapter 1 — The Man Who Owned Everything

“Tell him he’s lucky I’m acknowledging the request,” Jiang Tingyuan said. He kept his eyes on the tablet. “The answer is no. He can wait in the lobby until security deactivates his badge.”

No one around the boardroom table challenged him. The executives had spent the morning congratulating one another on a historic merger. Jiang let them enjoy the word. The company they had swallowed had been weakened months earlier: a refinancing deadline moved forward, two lenders persuaded to reconsider their exposure, and a succession dispute given just enough encouragement to become a war. By the time the board voted, every alternative cost more than surrender.

The champagne arrived. Jiang left it untouched.

He had learned young that people called a victory inevitable only after they could no longer see the work that made it so. Poverty had taught him to count what could be lost. Business had taught him to discover which loss a person feared most, then arrange the negotiation around it. He had built his empire by learning industries faster than his rivals expected, buying what he could not build, and dismantling what refused to be bought. He had no wife waiting at home, no children whose future softened his decisions. He had made that trade deliberately. He did not regret it.

On the ride back, he opened the cultivation novel he had been reading between meetings. The protagonist had just defeated a supposed mastermind whose plans collapsed the moment the hero needed a triumphant scene. Coincidence had become insight; luck had become destiny; every opponent had grown stupid on schedule.

“Predictable garbage,” Jiang muttered.

His driver glanced at him in the mirror and looked back at the road.

Jiang scrolled to the next chapter. The villain had supposedly spent decades preparing, yet had failed to account for a single obvious weakness. It was not even a clever trap. The plot had simply declared that the man must lose.

If Jiang had been placed in that world, he thought, he would have bought the mines before the hero found the ore, secured the sect's creditors before the elders noticed their debts, and made every rival pay for the privilege of underestimating him. He would not wait for destiny to hand him a weapon. He would acquire the people who made weapons, the roads that carried them, and the authority that decided who could use them.

The car entered an intersection.

Metal screamed.

A freight truck lurched across the lanes, its brakes gone. The driver swore and jerked the wheel, but there was nowhere to go. The truck struck the passenger side with a force that folded the door inward. Glass burst across Jiang's lap. The tablet flew from his hand. Something heavy crushed his chest, and the world narrowed to a pressure he could not bargain with.

He tried to draw breath. Nothing came.

There was no final insight, no dignified last thought. Only the absurdity of a life spent eliminating variables ending because one had slipped beyond his control.

Then even that thought was gone.






* * *






The first thing Jiang noticed was the smell.

Not burning rubber, hot metal, or the sharp chemical tang that had filled the car. This was sandalwood, faint and sweet. Beneath it lay the stillness of a room where nothing had recently been broken.

He opened his eyes.

A canopy hung above him, its fabric embroidered with gold thread. Lacquered furniture stood along the walls; a bronze incense burner floated a short distance above a carved stand. He stared at it, waiting for the trick to reveal itself. It did not. The scent drifted from the burner all the same.

Jiang sat up. Pain did not follow. His chest expanded without resistance. He looked at his hands and turned them over. Smooth skin, unmarked knuckles, no age spots. These were not the hands of the man who had built a technology empire across three decades of boardroom battles.

He remembered the truck. He remembered the crush of steel. He remembered dying.

His pulse quickened once, then settled. Panic would not make the room more comprehensible.

When he tried to understand where he was, another set of memories stirred—names, faces, fragments of family relationships, the weight of a title he had never held. They came without displacing his own life. Jiang remained Jiang Tingyuan; the information belonged to someone else, and yet it sat inside his mind with the unsettling familiarity of facts learned long ago.

Gu Tianxing. Young Master of the Gu Clan.

The name pulled another memory into focus: the cultivation novel he had been reading. Its world, its clans, its cultivation stages. Gu Tianxing had been one of its early villains, an arrogant young master who existed largely to be humiliated by the protagonist. He was loud enough to provoke a fight and foolish enough to believe his family name would protect him from the consequences. The protagonist needed a demonstration of a new technique. Gu Tianxing supplied the target.

Jiang looked toward the window. Beyond its lattice, he could see a slice of unfamiliar courtyard and roofline. No traffic. No electric hum. No city skyline.

He had not survived the crash in any ordinary sense. He had awakened in the world of a story he had finished reading.

The absurdity should have been enough to make him laugh. Instead, he found himself remembering the book's failures: the convenient discoveries, the rivals who forgot their own advantages, the supposedly brilliant villains who never prepared for obvious outcomes. He had read it as entertainment and dismissed it as nonsense. Now the nonsense had become his environment.

He knew the broad shape of the plot. The original protagonist would rise, find opportunities, and eventually confront the Azure Dragons, a calamity that threatened the world. Jiang remembered hidden treasures and turning points, but the details had never mattered enough to memorize. He had read quickly, irritated by the writing, confident that he would have done better.

The memory of Gu Tianxing was less complete than the novel's main arc. A disposable villain. A public humiliation. A death that arrived early enough for the protagonist's rise to feel satisfying.

How early?

He could not place the scene on a calendar. He could not even be sure which chapter he had reached when Gu Tianxing's part ended. The plot remained in his mind as a sequence of events without reliable dates, more like a report remembered after a long meeting than a document he could consult.

Jiang leaned back against the pillows and looked again at his unfamiliar hands.

He had been given a body, a name, and a place in a story that expected him to die. None of those facts told him what to do next. First he needed to establish where he was in the timeline, what this household expected from Gu Tianxing, and what the body could actually manage. Until then, any plan built on remembered plot would be guesswork dressed up as certainty.

He pushed the covers aside. The room was lavish. That did not mean he was safe.






* * *





Jiang crossed the room barefoot. The stone floor was cool beneath him. He flexed his fingers, rolled his shoulders, and tested his balance, then searched the desk for a letter or dated document. The drawers held writing tools and blank paper. The shelves offered ornaments and books he could not yet read with confidence. Nothing gave him a reliable place in the story's chronology.

He was tempted to work backward from the events he remembered. That was how mistakes began: a vague recollection became an assumption, the assumption became a deadline, and every later decision inherited its error. He had read the novel for amusement, not to build a survival plan. The protagonist's rise, Gu Tianxing's short-lived role, the hidden treasures, the Azure Dragon calamity—he could recall the broad outline. The order of smaller events was blurred.

Footsteps stopped outside the door.

Jiang waited. A light knock followed, cautious and formal. He opened the door to find a household attendant in plain service robes, hands folded in front of him. The man bowed without looking directly into Jiang's face.

“Young Master.”

The title confirmed what the borrowed memories had suggested. Jiang kept his expression neutral. He had no reason to announce that he was a stranger wearing the young master's face, and even less reason to imitate an arrogance he did not understand.

“What is today's date?”

“The fourteenth day of the current month, Young Master.”

“Which month? Which year?”

The attendant's eyes flicked up, then down again. “The fourth month of the Gu calendar, in the current year.”

It was an answer, though not a useful one yet. Jiang would need a reference point before those words could anchor anything. He could ask for the calendar, the clan's records, or a list of recent events. Better to start with what the attendant might know without having to speculate.

“What has happened in the Gu Clan recently?”

The man took a moment to consider. “The elders have been occupied with internal matters. The household has continued its ordinary business. Nothing has been announced that requires your immediate attention.”

A careful answer. Perhaps the attendant knew little; perhaps he had learned that young masters disliked being burdened with details. Jiang had spent enough years listening to executives conceal uncertainty behind polite phrases to recognize the habit, but he had no evidence that this man was concealing anything.

“What am I expected to do today?”

“Nothing has been given to me to report, Young Master.”

Jiang nodded. The attendant could tell him the date and repeat the limits of his own instructions. He could not explain the clan's politics or identify the point in the novel's timeline. Pressing him would only encourage guesses.

“That will be all.”

The attendant bowed and left. Jiang closed the door and looked at the blank paper on his desk. He had wanted a countdown; instead, he had learned how little the household expected him to ask. That, too, was information. A young master who had been properly engaged with his duties would presumably have a schedule, appointments, or people demanding his attention. Either Gu Tianxing's affairs were unusually quiet, or he had neglected them badly enough that no one expected anything.

Jiang wrote the date at the top of the page, then stopped. Without a known calendar or a confirmed event from the novel, the entry was a record, not an anchor.

He would need a better source—and a better understanding of the body he had inherited.





* * *





The attendant's footsteps faded down the corridor. Jiang remained by the door for a moment, listening to the ordinary sounds of a household continuing around him: distant conversation, a tray set down somewhere, the soft scrape of a door sliding shut. Nothing in it suggested that the young master had died and been replaced.

He returned to the mirror.

The face looking back at him was young, clean-skinned, and unfamiliar. Jiang raised a hand; the reflection did the same. The eyes were Gu Tianxing's, but the person studying them was not. He searched for some sign that the borrowed memories had changed him, that he had become a mixture of two people. There was no such sensation. His own life remained intact. The other man's knowledge sat alongside it, unevenly accessible.

He examined his shoulders and wrists, bent his knees, and shifted his weight. The body moved easily. No stiffness in the joints, no obvious injury. He took a few steps across the room and turned sharply. His balance held, though the movement felt different from what he expected. Youth had returned to him; ability had not been proven.

In business, a title could open a meeting, but it could not make a failing company profitable. “Young Master” might grant him authority over servants, access to resources, or protection from ordinary consequences. It might also make him a target for people who resented the family. He knew the label. He did not yet know its terms.

He looked around the room again. The silk canopy and lacquered furniture spoke of wealth. The floating incense burner spoke of a world whose rules he did not understand. The attendant's manner suggested that Gu Tianxing was accustomed to being obeyed, but not necessarily that he was respected. Jiang could not tell whether the household's quiet was deference or indifference.

He could demand a parade of explanations. He could also pretend to remember every custom and every face, then wait for someone to expose him. Neither option appealed. He had survived his first life by learning what he did not know before committing resources. The same discipline would have to serve him here, though the cost of a mistake might be much more immediate than a lost acquisition.

Jiang sat at the desk and wrote three headings: **remembered**, **observed**, **unknown**.

Under remembered, he noted the broad shape of the novel and the fact that Gu Tianxing died early. He left the timing blank. Under observed, he wrote the date he had been given, the unfamiliar body, the opulent room, and the attendant's limited answers. Under unknown, he listed the current point in the plot, the body's cultivation level, the household's expectations, and the people who might be dangerous.

The last column filled fastest.

He set down the brush. It would be easy to mistake the ability to name a problem for progress toward solving it. He needed sources, tests, and a way to compare results. The room could tell him nothing more. The attendant had reached the limits of his usefulness.

If this body belonged to a young master of a cultivation clan, the training grounds would be the place to begin. There, he could observe what other people considered normal, learn which abilities the body might possess, and find out whether Gu Tianxing's title rested on more than family connections.

Jiang rose, opened the door, and called to a servant passing the far end of the corridor.

“Take me to the eastern training courtyard.”

The servant stopped and bowed. “At once, Young Master.”

Jiang followed without asking the way twice.





* * *


The eastern courtyard opened beyond a broad stone threshold. Young men and women trained in scattered groups, their footwork raising fine dust from the paving. Wooden weapons cracked against one another. At the far end, two students repeated the same movement while an older instructor corrected the angle of a wrist.

The servant who had guided Jiang bowed and withdrew, visibly relieved to have completed the task without being asked anything else.

Jiang stayed at the entrance long enough to see how the courtyard worked. Instructors watched the students who needed correction; the more experienced trainees practiced without supervision. No one appeared to be waiting for Gu Tianxing. That changed as soon as people noticed him.

A pair of trainees missed the rhythm of their exchange. One lowered his practice sword and glanced at his partner. Another whispered something that made the girl beside him look away. The training did not stop, but its rhythm shifted around Jiang, like a room full of people trying to carry on a conversation after a powerful man had entered.

He noted the reactions without deciding what they meant. Fear, dislike, and respect could all produce lowered eyes. He would need more than a bow to tell them apart.

A youth near the closest practice area stepped forward. He was lean, perhaps a little younger than Jiang’s new body, and held a wooden blade worn smooth around the grip. His salute was respectful enough to satisfy etiquette, though his eyes stayed on Jiang’s face.

“Young Master. Are you here to train?”

“I’m here to learn what this body can do.”

The youth hesitated. “Would you like me to test you?”

“What would that involve?”

“A basic exchange. Nothing serious. I can show you where you stand.”

“Who decides what counts as basic?”

The boy’s fingers shifted on the hilt. “The instructor sets the exercises. For a first exchange, we use a simple attack and a defensive response.”

“And if one of us gets hurt?”

“We stop. The practice blades are blunted.”

“That answers what the weapons are for. It doesn’t answer whether you know how to control one.”

A few nearby trainees had begun listening openly. The youth noticed them and straightened. Jiang could not tell whether the boy had volunteered out of courtesy, curiosity, or a desire to show the others how little he feared the young master.

“Show me the exercise first,” Jiang said. “One attack at a time. Start slowly, and explain what you’re testing.”

The youth looked toward an older trainee standing beside the weapon rack. The older boy gave a slight nod, but did not intervene. Permission, then—or a decision that any embarrassment would be Jiang’s own.

The youth stepped into the open space and raised his blade. He adjusted his stance once, then again, trying to settle his feet under Jiang’s attention.

Jiang watched the small corrections. The boy was nervous, but not so nervous that he had forgotten his training. That was a useful distinction. A frightened opponent could be careless; a cautious one might be more dangerous than he looked.

“Ready?” the youth asked.

“Proceed.”

The wooden blade began to move.

## Chapter 2 — The First Measure

The wooden blade came toward Jiang’s shoulder in a clean, direct line. He saw the strike begin and considered retreating, turning, or blocking. Before he chose, one foot slid diagonally. His shoulders turned; his right hand guided the blade aside without catching it. The weapon passed close enough to stir his sleeve.

The youth froze. So did several trainees watching nearby.

Jiang looked at his own hand. He had not planned the movement. He had barely understood the attack before his body answered it.

“Again,” he said. “Same speed.”

The boy reset his stance, more carefully now. “Watch my shoulders, Young Master. The weapon follows the turn.” He demonstrated the movement slowly, then struck again.

Jiang watched the shoulder and tried to repeat his earlier response deliberately. He moved too late. The blade tapped his upper arm.

The youth immediately lowered his weapon. “Forgive me, Young Master.”

“You followed the instructions I gave you. Continue.”

The answer seemed to unsettle him more than anger would have. He glanced at Jiang’s face, searching for a trap, then returned to his stance.

“Show me the attack in stages,” Jiang said. “Don’t change anything unless I ask.”

The youth shifted his weight, turned his shoulders, and brought the blade around. Jiang asked him to repeat it. On the third demonstration, the boy’s weight shifted earlier than Jiang expected.

“That was different.”

“I was preparing to strike, Young Master.”

“Then preparation and commitment are separate. If I react to the first, you can still change direction.”

“Yes, Young Master.”

Jiang had identified a principle, not mastered a skill. He tried again, moved too early, and lost his balance. The youth halted the blade rather than exploit the mistake.

“Too soon,” the boy said, then stiffened. “Forgive me. I should not correct you without permission.”

“Correct an error when you see one. I asked you to show me what happens, not flatter me.”

The boy’s expression changed. He had expected Gu Tianxing to be arrogant, not curious. Around them, the other trainees had stopped pretending not to watch.

Jiang rolled his shoulder. The tap had barely hurt. What mattered was the difference between the two attempts. The first movement had been precise; the second, consciously commanded, had failed. Whatever had guided the first response was part of the body’s history, not a skill Jiang could yet summon at will.

“Again. This time, tell me when you shift your weight.”

The youth demonstrated the attack in a slower rhythm. Jiang watched his feet, then his hips, then the turn of his shoulders. He tried to coordinate his own movement with the change in balance. The first attempt was clumsy. The second kept him upright. On the third, he cleared the blade’s path but turned too far and exposed his side.

“Would that count as a successful evasion?” Jiang asked.

“Only if the other person stops after the first strike.”

“Then it isn’t one.”

The boy gave a small, involuntary smile before remembering himself. “No, Young Master.”

“Good. We agree on something.”

Jiang ended the exchange before curiosity turned into a contest. He had no reason to prove himself to the watching trainees, and no basis for claiming he had learned to fight. He had learned that the body could respond faster than his conscious mind, that he could not reliably reproduce the response, and that careful repetition made a small difference.

“What is your name?”

“Lin Wei, Young Master.”

“Take me to the instructor responsible for this courtyard. I want an honest assessment of my cultivation and training. Don’t tell him what answer I expect.”

Lin Wei bowed. His earlier caution remained, but the fear in it had loosened into uncertainty. “As you wish.”

Jiang followed him out of the practice space. Behind them, the courtyard gradually resumed its rhythm. He had gained no new rank and no dependable combat skill. He had, however, found the first discrepancy between what Gu Tianxing’s body knew and what Jiang could consciously command. That was worth investigating.

* * *







Jiang let Lin Wei walk half a pace ahead of him, close enough to guide him and far enough to preserve the appearance of deference. The boy kept his shoulders rigid and his eyes lowered.

“You said I had trained here before,” Jiang said. “How often?”

“Not regularly, Young Master. Sometimes several days in a row, then not again for a while.”

“Was I improving?”

Lin Wei's steps faltered. “I cannot judge your potential, Young Master.”

“I didn't ask about my potential. I asked what you observed.”

The distinction seemed to help. “Your foundation was good. You learned the basic forms faster than some of the others. But when the instructor corrected you, you would become annoyed. Sometimes you left early.”

Jiang considered the answer. It matched the body’s instinctive movement without proving mastery. Gu Tianxing had been talented enough to learn, proud enough to resist correction, and inconsistent enough to remain mediocre in practice. That was a plausible explanation, not yet a complete one.

“Did the instructor complain about me?”

“I heard him say that talent is wasted if it is not trained. I don't know if he meant you specifically.”

“Did I punish anyone for correcting me?”

Lin Wei went silent.

Jiang glanced at him. “You may answer.”

“I don't know what you would consider punishment, Young Master.”

That was not an answer, but it was an important boundary. The boy feared the question more than he trusted the promise of safety. Jiang would not learn the truth by demanding confidence he had not earned.

“Then we will leave that question for now. When I ask about something you did not witness, say so. When you did witness it, tell me what happened. Do not guess what I want to hear.”

“Yes, Young Master.”

They reached the far side of the courtyard, where an instructor in dark training robes was correcting two older trainees. He had a broad frame, a still posture, and the patient expression of a man who had repeated the same correction hundreds of times. He looked up as Jiang approached. The trainees straightened immediately.

Lin Wei bowed. “Instructor. Young Master Gu wishes to speak with you.”

The instructor returned the bow, measured and formal. “Young Master. I was told you had come to observe.”

“I came to learn,” Jiang said. “I want to understand what this body can do and what I am expected to know.”

The instructor paused. It was only a fraction of a second, but Jiang noticed the pause and the way the man's gaze searched his face.

“You wish to resume training?”

“I wish to begin properly. First, tell me what I already have. Then tell me what I lack.”

“That is a reasonable request.” The instructor gestured toward a low stone platform beside the yard. “Sit, Young Master. We should begin with your cultivation, not your fighting.”

Jiang sat. Lin Wei remained nearby until the instructor dismissed him with a nod. The boy bowed and withdrew, still glancing back once before returning to the trainees.

“Before we begin,” Jiang said, “one question. Is there a reason I should already know these things?”

The instructor studied him. “Yes. You have received instruction for years.”

“Then either I have forgotten it, or I was never taught it properly. We can determine which later. For now, teach me as you would teach someone who needs the foundation explained.”

The instructor's guarded expression did not disappear, but it became more attentive.

“As you wish, Young Master.”

Jiang had learned something already. People around Gu Tianxing expected him to be arrogant, not curious. He could exploit that expectation, but only if he understood what it concealed. For now, the instructor was the better source.







* * *







“Cultivation begins with the body, the breath, the circulation of qi, and the ability to guide it without injuring yourself,” the instructor said. “Combat is only one use of what a cultivator develops.”

“Define qi.”

“The vital energy cultivators draw from the world, refine, and circulate through the body. With training, it strengthens the body and supports techniques beyond ordinary physical ability.”

“Through the blood?”

“No. Meridians are the channels through which qi circulates. They connect through internal pathways and converge around the dantian, where refined qi is gathered and stored. That is the simple explanation. The details take years.”

Jiang ordered the terms: energy, channels, reservoir. In the novel, they had been labels for dramatic breakthroughs. Here, they described a system with practical limits he would need to understand.

“And the ranks?”

“The Gu Clan teaches the standard progression. Body Tempering conditions the flesh and bones. Qi Gathering begins when a cultivator senses and draws qi into the body, then learns to circulate and refine it. Foundation Establishment follows when the foundation is stable enough to support a complete cultivation cycle. There are higher realms, but you should master the basics first.”

“Which stage am I in?”

The instructor looked at him. “You should already know that, Young Master.”

“I don't.”

Silence settled between them. Jiang did not bluff or offer an excuse. If the instructor believed this was a test, he could say so. If Jiang tried to fake knowledge, the deception would eventually be exposed.

After a moment, the instructor inclined his head. “I will take you at your word.”

He guided Jiang through a simple breathing exercise and told him to relax his attention below the navel. At first, Jiang felt only the air in his lungs, the stone beneath his feet, and the tension in his shoulders. When he tried to force a sensation, nothing came. When he relaxed, a faint pressure surfaced in his lower abdomen—not quite heat, not quite touch.

“There,” the instructor said. “You sensed it. Do not force qi through your meridians without guidance. Poorly controlled circulation can injure you.”

“What is my stage?”

“Third layer of Qi Gathering. Your foundation is sound, and your meridians respond normally. For someone your age, your talent is respectable.”

“Then why have I not advanced?”

“Your training has been inconsistent. You have not advanced recently, as far as I know. Talent does not refine qi on its own.”

The body was not an empty vessel. It had a foundation, talent, and years of instruction behind it. The former owner had treated those advantages as guarantees rather than resources.

“Show me the defensive step from the exchange,” Jiang said. “Slowly. Explain the weight shift and the breath.”

The instructor demonstrated. Jiang copied him and got it wrong: knees locked, shoulders turning too early, weight drifting beyond his base. The instructor corrected each fault. Jiang understood the words but could not make the parts flow together.

“Again,” the instructor said. “Do not imitate the shape alone. Feel where your weight rests.”

Jiang remembered the blade approaching and his body moving without permission. He had tried to reconstruct the response by commanding each joint. This time, he settled his posture, followed the instructor's breathing, and stopped micromanaging the movement.

His left foot shifted. His weight followed. His torso turned just enough to clear an imaginary strike, and his balance held.

It was the same evasion, though less clean than the first.

“Your body remembers the movement,” the instructor said.

“My body produced it. I still don't understand why.”

“That understanding comes with practice.”

“Then we'll measure the practice.”

The instructor gave him a long look. “Most students want to feel more qi. You want to know why a movement succeeds.”

“I want to know what I can reproduce and what I cannot.”

Jiang repeated the step. On the next attempt, his heel turned too far and his balance wavered. The third was better. He did not confuse improvement with mastery; he had found a method for practicing, not proof that he could fight.

“Review my training records,” he said. “Send me the introductory manual and the exercises I can safely repeat alone. Include the warning signs that mean I should stop.”

“Yes, Young Master.”

“And tell me plainly when I am wrong. I have no use for instruction that protects my pride at the cost of my progress.”

For the first time, the instructor's expression shifted toward approval. “Then you may learn quickly.”

Jiang rose from the platform. His rank had not changed, and his command of qi remained uncertain. But he had a process: observe, isolate a variable, repeat, compare, and record. Cultivation was unfamiliar; disciplined inquiry was not.

He would not become powerful in three days. He could stop wasting them.







* * *







Jiang left the courtyard with instructions, not a miracle. That distinction suited him.

Back in his quarters, he ordered writing materials, a meal, and the person responsible for his household schedule. The attendant hesitated at the last request.

“Would you like me to ask the steward for the household register, Young Master?”

“I would like the person who maintains it. If the steward controls it, bring the steward. If someone else does, bring that person.”

“Yes, Young Master.”

“And bring any recent messages or invitations. Do not discard anything because it seemed unimportant.”

When the attendant withdrew, Jiang sat at the desk and divided a sheet into three columns: **remembered**, **observed**, and **unknown**.

Under remembered, he wrote the broad facts from the novel. Gu Tianxing died early, perhaps around the fifth to tenth chapter. The protagonist survived and eventually became powerful enough to confront the Azure Dragons. Jiang remembered opportunities and turning points, but not a reliable calendar. He had read the story for entertainment, not as a forensic record.

Under observed, he recorded the date—the fourteenth day of the current month—his rank at the third layer of Qi Gathering, the instructor's assessment of his foundation, and his history of inconsistent training.

The unknown column was longer. Which month was this? How far had the protagonist progressed? Had Gu Tianxing already offended him? Which remembered scenes belonged to this point in the timeline? How much of the original sequence remained intact now that someone else occupied this body?

He did not promote guesses into facts. A wrong date was a correctable error; a plan built around it could be fatal.

A knock interrupted him. The attendant returned with a household register and an older clerk carrying a wooden case.

“Your household records, Young Master,” the clerk said. “The steward sent what could be gathered at short notice.”

“Who maintains the schedule of my obligations?”

“I do, under the steward's authority.”

“Then tell me what I am expected to do over the next ten days. Prioritize clan gatherings, public appearances, training, and visitors.”

The clerk opened the register. Most entries were routine. Jiang dismissed what could be delegated and listened closely when the clerk reached a notice bearing the seal of the Gu Clan's inner administration.

“The young masters and young ladies are expected at the family assembly in three days,” the clerk said. “Your attendance is required.”

Jiang's pen stopped.

The phrase meant little by itself. The timing did. He remembered a gathering of the Gu Clan's younger generation in the novel, a scene he had skimmed because it seemed to exist chiefly to display a minor villain's arrogance. He could not recall every detail, but Gu Tianxing had been present. Soon afterward, the protagonist's rise had intersected with the clan's affairs.

“Who will attend?”

“The younger generation, the elders responsible for their instruction, and invited guests. I can retrieve the full list.”

“Do so. I want the purpose of the assembly, the order of proceedings, and any role assigned to me. Find out whether I am expected to speak, demonstrate my cultivation, or simply attend.”

“Yes, Young Master.”

“Also check whether any outside disciple, visitor, or recent arrival is connected to the assembly. Quietly. I want records, not speculation.”

The clerk bowed and withdrew.

Jiang looked at his three columns again. The assembly might be the event he remembered, or it might merely resemble it. He would not let a familiar label trick him into believing he had found his place in the story.

Still, three days was a deadline, and deadlines could be used.

He needed the guest list, the proceedings, a clearer understanding of his current capabilities, and facts about the people likely to attend. He would compare those facts with his recollection only after he had them. If the assembly matched the novel, the match could be useful. If it did not, the difference would be useful too.

He set his pen down. In his first life, he had built an empire by acquiring information before competitors understood its value. Here, the information was scattered among attendants, clan records, cultivation manuals, and the damaged memory of a story he had never expected to inhabit.

He would gather it, sort it, and act only when the evidence justified the risk.

The next knock might bring the first fact that mattered.

## Chapter 3 — Three Days to the Assembly

The records arrived before noon.

Jiang had expected a stack of ledgers thick enough to bury a clerk beneath them. Instead, the steward's representative brought a slim register, a bundle of training notes, and a stitched booklet whose cover read *Foundations of Qi Cultivation*. The selection was either a sign that Gu Tianxing's affairs were simple or that the household had decided he did not need much.

Jiang set the booklet aside and opened the register.

His training record was brief. Several periods of regular instruction, followed by absences, complaints from instructors, and long gaps in practice. His cultivation had reached the third layer of Qi Gathering, then stalled. There were no injuries, no failed breakthroughs, and no alarming irregularities in his meridians. The written assessment matched what the instructor had told him: a good foundation, respectable talent, wasted through neglect.

Useful. Not flattering, but useful.

He turned to the manual. The first pages explained how to sense qi, settle the breath, and avoid disrupting its circulation through the meridians. He read slowly, marking terms he could not yet connect to experience. It was not enough to read instructions and assume he could perform them. Still, the words gave shape to the faint sensation he had felt in his lower abdomen. For the first time, the body's unfamiliar responses had a framework he could test.

The guest list came last.

Jiang scanned the names of the clan's younger generation, the elders overseeing the assembly, and several visitors from affiliated households. Most meant nothing to him. Then he reached a note about a young outer disciple expected to attend under a branch family's sponsorship.

The name was **Shen Yan**.

Jiang's fingers stopped.

Not because the name itself mattered. Because he remembered it.

The novel's opening arc had spent several pages on the Gu Clan assembly. Jiang had nearly forgotten the scene: an outsider with no standing, invited to observe proceedings, treated as an amusing inconvenience by the original Gu Tianxing. The young master had mocked the boy's background and challenged his right to speak. When Shen Yan refused to lower his head, Gu Tianxing had escalated the insult into a public confrontation.

The memory sharpened in fragments. A broken token. Laughter from the younger generation. A challenge that should have ended with a humiliating lesson for a nobody.

Instead, it had been the first time the protagonist made the Gu Clan notice him.

Jiang leaned back, eyes fixed on the page. He could not recover every line of the scene, nor could he swear the details were exact. He did remember what followed: Shen Yan's refusal to accept the humiliation, Gu Tianxing's insistence on forcing the issue, and a confrontation that transformed a petty insult into a grudge. The original young master had not died at the assembly itself. His public cruelty had put him on the protagonist's list, and the fatal encounter came soon afterward.

That was the shape of the trap.

Not fate. Not a mysterious calamity. A chain of decisions, beginning with an insult that Gu Tianxing had been too arrogant to leave unanswered.

Jiang looked again at the date. Three days until the assembly. If Shen Yan was the same person from the novel—and if the story's sequence still held—then the event was not merely a possible landmark. It was the first known point at which Jiang could cut the chain.

He took up his pen and wrote two lines beneath his earlier notes.

**Likely plot anchor: Shen Yan attends the family assembly.**  
**Primary objective: prevent the public confrontation without revealing foreknowledge.**

He paused, then underlined *likely*. He would not replace one kind of foolishness with another by treating memory as evidence beyond question. The guest list was evidence that Shen Yan was expected to attend. The rest was recollection, incomplete and potentially distorted by whatever had changed when Jiang took over this body.

He closed the register and reached for the manual again. Three days was not enough to become a master, but it was enough to prepare: learn what Gu Tianxing was expected to do, understand the limits of his current cultivation, and ensure that no one could maneuver him into repeating the original young master's worst mistake.

The original Gu Tianxing had believed that status meant he could force any situation to bend around him.

Jiang would use that status for something more valuable.

He would make sure the assembly happened—and that the story did not happen the same way.






* * *



Jiang sent for the eastern courtyard’s instructor before the afternoon had passed. The man arrived with a practice blade and a guarded expression.

“You asked for further instruction, Young Master?”

“Not another lesson in sensing qi. I know the basics you showed me. I want to understand what I can safely rely on in front of an audience.”

“At the assembly?”

“I don’t know whether I’ll be asked to demonstrate, challenged, or left alone. I won’t prepare for one possibility and call it certainty.”

“Three days is not enough to advance a cultivation stage through ordinary practice,” the instructor said. “Forcing one would be reckless. You can improve control and correct wasteful movement.”

“Then test me.”

The instructor raised the practice blade and asked Jiang to repeat the defensive step from the morning. Jiang watched the weapon and turned too soon. The blade stopped against his shoulder.

“Again.”

On the next attempt, he waited too long. The instructor tapped his ribs.

“You’re watching the weapon. By then, the attack is already underway. Watch the body that commits to it.”

Jiang asked him to repeat the motion at half speed. He tracked the shift of the instructor’s hips, the turn of his shoulder, and the moment the weapon followed. On the third attempt, Jiang moved late but kept his balance.

“Better,” the instructor said. “Still slow.”

“What changed?”

“You stopped trying to copy the blade. You moved when the weight shifted.”

Jiang tried again. He cleared the line of attack without crossing his feet, then lost the movement when the instructor changed the angle. He accepted the next tap without complaint.

“Can I reproduce it under pressure?”

“Not reliably. Your stance is improving, and your body responds well to correction. But you have no dependable combat rhythm yet. Against a trained opponent, your first instinct may save you once. You cannot build a plan around it.”

It was the first assessment Jiang had received that included a limit he could use. He asked for three drills in writing: the defensive step, the breathing posture, and a basic movement to recover his balance. No advanced circulation and no attempt to force more qi through his meridians.

“Practice them separately,” the instructor said. “Stop if you feel pain, dizziness, or an irregular pulse of qi. Repetition is not readiness.”

“I won’t mistake it for readiness.”

Jiang turned to the assembly. “Who can call for a demonstration?”

“The presiding elder controls the proceedings. An elder overseeing a junior can request one. A junior may ask, but has no right to demand that another person perform.”

“Can a refusal be treated as an insult?”

“That depends on how it is presented—and who is listening.”

“Then etiquette is part of the contest.”

“It often is.”

Jiang asked whether his name appeared beside any assigned demonstration. The instructor had heard of no assignment, but agreed to check with the steward. Jiang also asked him to keep the session private. The courtyard had already supplied enough witnesses to his inexperience.

After the instructor left, Jiang returned to his notes about Shen Yan. At first he had written that he should prevent the remembered confrontation. He drew a line through the sentence.

Avoiding the insult might spare him a grudge. It might also throw away one of the few events he could anticipate with any confidence. He would follow the story to the confrontation, but he would not repeat the original Gu Tianxing’s mistake: provoking a person without knowing what stood behind him or what the room would permit him to do.

Shen Yan would still be humiliated. Jiang would make certain he had something to support the act—cultivation he could demonstrate, rules he could invoke, or facts that left the other boy with no clean answer. If he could secure none of those, he would know the limits before he opened his mouth.

He wrote beneath Shen Yan’s name: **Prepare for the confrontation. Do not bluff about what I can do.**

Three days would not make him a master. They could make him harder to surprise.



* * *







The household clerk returned near the end of the afternoon with a revised schedule, three copied notices, and the cautious look of a man who had been told to answer questions without creating new ones.

Jiang set aside his cultivation manual. “Start with the assembly. What is decided, and what is still customary?”

“The assembly is called to review the younger generation's progress and receive the invited guests, Young Master. The presiding elder will open the proceedings. After that, several juniors may be asked to report on their training. Demonstrations are possible, but they are not listed as compulsory.”

“Possible by whose decision?”

“The presiding elder's, or at the request of an elder overseeing a participant.”

“So the schedule does not guarantee that I will be asked to demonstrate.”

“No, Young Master.”

“Nor does it guarantee that I won't.”

The clerk lowered his eyes. “That is correct.”

Jiang read the copied notice. Its language was formal and deliberately broad. The assembly was a family occasion, but also a public arrangement of rank: elders watched, juniors performed, and invited guests were reminded of who possessed the authority to judge them. A careless scene could become a spectacle simply because the room was built to reward one.

“Who selected the invited guests?”

“The steward's office received the names from the branches and the elders responsible for external relations. I can tell you what the register records, but I was not involved in selecting them.”

“Then tell me what it records about Shen Yan.”

The clerk hesitated. Not long enough to be theatrical; just long enough to show he understood that the name had weight.

“Shen Yan is listed as an outer disciple attending under a branch family's sponsorship. The entry confirms his invitation, but it does not explain the sponsor's reasons. I have requested the original submission.”

“Good. Do not fill the gap with what people say about him.”

“Yes, Young Master.”

“What do we know about his standing at the assembly?”

“Less than we know about the clan's own juniors. He has no listed speaking role. His attendance is permitted; it does not give him a formal place in the proceedings.”

Jiang tapped the edge of the notice once. “That is a distinction worth keeping.”

He could already see the shape of the original scene more clearly—not its dialogue, which remained blurred, but its machinery. Gu Tianxing had apparently treated Shen Yan's presence as an insult to his own rank. He had converted a guest list into a personal challenge, then escalated when the other boy refused to behave as expected. The remembered details might be wrong. The underlying mistake was plain enough: he had confused the power to make a scene with the power to control its consequences.

“Find the original sponsorship request,” Jiang said. “I want the stated purpose of the invitation, who submitted it, and whether any elder has assigned Shen Yan a role. If the record says nothing, report that it says nothing.”

“I will.”

“And the order of proceedings?”

“I have brought the latest copy.”

“Who can amend it?”

“The presiding elder.”

“Can a junior demand a demonstration?”

“Not as a right, Young Master. A junior could ask, but whether the request is entertained depends on the elder.”

Jiang nodded. The room had rules, even if they were flexible. That made it less dangerous than a room with no rules—and more dangerous than one where everyone followed them. If he wanted the humiliation to land, he needed an audience and a defensible position. If he wanted the consequences to remain manageable, he needed to know which authority could stop him.

He had no intention of inventing a grievance, making a threat he could not enforce, or relying on a breakthrough that three days of practice could not provide. His cultivation was still the third layer of Qi Gathering. He could improve his control, perhaps make his movements less clumsy, but pretending he had become a different fighter would invite a test he might fail.

That left the assets he actually possessed: Gu Tianxing's name, the formal structure of the assembly, and whatever the records could establish about Shen Yan's invitation. Status was not strength, but it could determine who was allowed to speak, who had to answer, and whose conduct would be judged in public.

“Bring me the sponsorship record when it arrives,” Jiang said. “Until then, no one is to be questioned on my behalf outside the proper channels. I want information, not a rumor that I have taken an interest in him.”

The clerk bowed and withdrew with the notices.

Jiang returned to the sheet divided into remembered, observed, and unknown. Under observed, he added the assembly's discretionary proceedings and Shen Yan's lack of a listed speaking role. Under unknown, he wrote the sponsor's identity, the reason for the invitation, and the presiding elder's likely response to a public challenge. He did not move any of them into the first column.

Then he drew a line beneath the three entries and wrote a fourth note in the margin:

**Do not confuse the opening move with control of the outcome.**

He could follow the story without surrendering himself to it. The humiliation would be deliberate; the preparation would be real. Before the assembly, he would know what the records could tell him, practice what his body could actually reproduce, and decide how much risk the available leverage could support.

For now, the most valuable fact was not what Shen Yan had done.

It was what Jiang still did not know.
